## Summary

The paper's restatement of Lebesgue's fundamental theorem of calculus on [a, b], which is what our work drew on, stands. We did not check the proofs of the paper's main results, Theorems 3.0.2 and 3.0.5, and this comment says nothing about them. Our one recorded finding concerns a **background statement** in Sec. 3: the paper's summary of the Henstock-Kurzweil (HK) fundamental theorem. As worded, it requires only that f′ exist almost everywhere, and the Cantor function is a counterexample. The main results do not use this statement. It is a discrepancy, not a refutation. We also note, in passing, one sentence of the abstract that disagrees with the body.

## Finding

### p. 5, item 3: the Henstock-Kurzweil fundamental theorem as stated (discrepancy)

- **The paper states** (p. 5): "Every derivative is HK-integrable, and for any function f, if f′ exists almost everywhere, then ∫_a^b f′(x) dx = f(b) − f(a) in the Henstock-Kurzweil sense. The HK integral thus restores the full converse of the FTC."
- **What we find:**
  - The first clause is correct for derivatives that exist everywhere.
  - The second clause is not. Take the Cantor function on [0, 1]. It is continuous and non-decreasing, its derivative exists and is 0 almost everywhere, and f(1) − f(0) = 1. So ∫₀¹ f′ = 0 ≠ 1, in the Lebesgue and HK senses alike.
  - The standard HK form needs f continuous on [a, b] and differentiable everywhere, or everywhere except on a countable set. Under those conditions f′ is HK-integrable and ∫_a^b f′ = f(b) − f(a).
  - The paper's focus, stated in the next paragraph, is Lebesgue's theorem. The HK remark is not used afterwards.
- **Shown by:** `research/warp-drive/docket67-raw/shards/shard06.json`, key `ftc-absolute-continuity`, `later_literature` (the Banerjee row); the Cantor-function computation in `research/warp-drive/docket67-raw/rederive/ftc-absolute-continuity.py` (check T4 uses p_r = Cantor(r), with the exact Cantor-measure moments, as a function whose derivative vanishes almost everywhere while the function itself does not stay constant).
- **Class:** discrepancy.

### In passing: the abstract on L¹_G and L¹ (discrepancy; noted in the verification re-read, not in the audit record)

- **The paper states** (abstract, p. 1): the derivative of an AC(ℝ) function must belong to "L¹_G(ℝ) (a strict subspace of L¹(ℝ))".
- **What we find:** the inclusion runs the other way. The constant function 1 lies in L¹_G(ℝ), since ∫_K 1 = μ(K) < ∞ for every set K of finite measure (Notation 2.0.4), but not in L¹(ℝ). The body says this itself: on p. 2, "the condition F′ ∈ L¹(ℝ) is too strong, since even the simple function f(x) = x belongs to AC(ℝ) but satisfies f′ ∉ L¹(ℝ)", and on p. 6 L¹_G is called "a strict subspace of L¹_loc(ℝ)". The abstract's "L¹(ℝ)" reads as a slip for "L¹_loc(ℝ)".
- **Shown by:** the one-line example above, read against pp. 1, 2, 3 and 6 of v2 at source (alphaXiv, 2026-10-04).
- **Class:** discrepancy (abstract against body).

## What stands

- **Lebesgue's fundamental theorem of calculus** for absolutely continuous functions on [a, b], as restated on pp. 1 and 5.
- **Example 3.0.1** (p. 6). For f(x) = √|x| extended with period 2, the intervals (2i, 2i + δ/i²) have total length below 2δ while Σ√δ/i diverges, so f is in AC_loc(ℝ) but not in AC(ℝ), as stated.
