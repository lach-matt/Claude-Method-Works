# California Sovereign Infrastructure: the case in brief

**Matthew Lach, Independent Researcher, 2026**

*The accompaniment to the full proposal. Every figure here is the proposal's, labelled mid / critical, and the
proposal carries its source and its case.*

## The problem

- California's residential load peaks after sunset and in summer, and the grid serves those hours with gas and
  imports at rising prices; a household's generation charge has reached about $1,177 a year.
- The San Joaquin Valley's groundwater is overdrafted by 1.8–2.5 million acre-feet a year under a statute that
  requires the deficit to close; the coast has no new firm water source in service.
- Neither scarcity yields to demand reduction at scale, and every supply that can meet them is capital-intensive,
  first-of-a-kind, or both. This program addresses the two together, and prices both honestly.

## The ask

- One state-owned program under one Authority, two severable projects, funded once by general-obligation bonds and
  never again by the treasury.
- Title I makes the electricity for three million households, 18.1 TWh a year, growing with population.
- Title II makes their water: 15–17 desalination modules, 0.75–0.83 million acre-feet a year, delivered year-round.
- Title III joins them so each helps the other and neither can drag the other down.
- **Decision requested:** fund the studies and surveys now, $213–463 million, and know within
  2–4 years whether the rest is worth $67–114 billion.

## What it builds

- **Helios-3.** Concentrating solar on three desert nodes — Mojave, Imperial, the Westside — with falling-particle
  receivers and supercritical-CO₂ turbines instead of salt and steam, so the same sun makes half again as much power
  (sun to socket 0.168 for the salt tower, 0.252 at mid).
- Daytime load goes straight from photovoltaics without touching a mirror; the mirror field is sized for the night;
  the particle store carries the evening peak.
- As sized at mid: 18.1 million m² of mirror on 14 towers, a 2,620 MWe block, 2,894 MW of PV. At critical: 30.9 million m²,
  24 towers, 2,620 MWe, 3,101 MW. The adopted route then grows the field by a quarter and the block by half to
  close the evening.
- **Aqua-Sovereign.** Seawater reverse osmosis on retired coastal power plants, brine back through the permitted
  outfall at Ocean Plan concentration, no minerals sold, no zero-liquid-discharge.
- **The joinder.** Title I's summer surplus lifts the water to an elevated reservoir; every evening of the year it
  comes down through pump-turbines to close Title I's peak. Water for the Westside and the coast, and the evening's
  last shortfall closed by the water on its way down.
- Together they leave **0.6% / 0.7%** of the load to the grid, against 13% / 13% with neither.

- Every figure carries its contingency, 15% at mid and 30% at critical, and a one-year debt-service reserve.
- Every study and survey is the first line, $213 / 463 million, priced and scheduled before a mirror is ordered.
- A salt-tower plant of this size costs $46.1–71.5 B on the built record and does not pay for itself; this
  program builds a different plant and prices it from the record, not from a promise.

## What a household pays

- Today: **$1,177** a year to the utility for generation.
- On this program while the bonds are paid: **$1,036** at mid, **$1,682** at critical
  ($172 / 279 per MWh).
- After the bonds retire: **$190 / 263** a year, the running cost alone, for as long as the plant stands.
- 81% / 85% of the plant's bill is the build. Once built, the state owns a supply whose running cost is a fraction of today's bill.
- Water: **$3,044 / 4,830** an acre-foot at cost recovery, in the band Carlsbad delivers ($2,700–2,900), on its own rate and account.
- No further public money after the build: the bill carries debt service and O&M, the treasury carries nothing.

## Why it holds up

- Every number comes from a calculation that can be re-run, printed at a middle case and at a critical case where
  every uncertain constant sits at its adverse end. The design is held to critical; mid is margin.
- The plant is sized to a requirement, three million households growing 1.35–1.81× over the term, and the
  requirement is met before a kilowatt-hour is sold outside the state.
- It takes no developer's margin and no state subsidy after the build, and keeps the federal storage credit where
  statute allows.
- Every technology in it is on a register of 49 published studies and plants, each with a status and a source, and
  a recommended further study on a dated ladder.
- It mines nothing new, consumes no cooling water, and avoids 7.24 / 6.34 million tonnes of CO₂ a year with the load closed.
- About 21,054 construction jobs at peak and 1,901 / 1,214 permanent on the nodes, plus 601 / 664 at the water modules.

## Where

- Title I: the Mojave (Kramer Junction), Imperial (Desert Center) and the Westside. At mid the Westside ties the
  Mojave for first on land, nexus and grid; at critical the Mojave leads on seismic margin. Land 45,361 / 66,339 acres, with
  fallowed Westside farmland covering a whole node.
- Title II: eight retired or retiring coastal plants screened on seven terms; the Oxnard plain (Ormond Beach, Mandalay)
  and Moss Landing lead; Huntington Beach carries its 2022 denial as a FAIL; no site is met on every term, so a site
  study is always owed.

## What could stop it

- No plant of this kind has run at scale; the pilot comes first and the salt block stands behind it.
- The plant must deliver its modelled output, or the bill rises; no commercial salt tower has yet delivered its design.
- The coastal permits are the longest studies on the path and set the critical schedule.
- At the critical case a household pays more than today for one bond term. The proposal says so on every page,
  because a plan that hides its worst case is not one that can be built.

## The decision

- Fund the studies now, for $213–463 million, and know within 2–4 years whether the rest is worth
  $67–114 billion.
- Nothing irreversible is bought before the receiver has passed its test.
