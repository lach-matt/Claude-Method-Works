## VERSION B (revised) — the null as the moment the object made itself known

**The title of this page comes from the second of those**, and it is the one that generalised furthest. It began with a null. Early on, working in fourteen dimensions, I had computed a quantity that came out null, and I insisted the null could not be broken — that nothing was there to define, because nothing in the cell would ever violate it. The author's correction was that a null is still definable: its definition does not come from inside the cell, but from an observation made outside it. It took that exchange — **nothing is something definable** — for the object this book is about to make itself known at all. The null was the first sign of it; reading the null from outside is what turned it into something with a definition. That is where the page's title comes from.

The same lesson, stated formally, is this. I had computed that a particular identity, w = V·e (it multiplies two derived quantities, V = 4ν/3 and e, to give a third), detects no error in the data. The correction was that a relation which *cannot* be violated by data is not empty — **it is defending something else**, namely the derivation that produced it. A relation data can never break is checking that a quantity reached one way agrees with the same quantity reached another way. That single reframing produced the split running through Chapter 16: ⅅ_phys, which checks the book's numbers against physical relations from outside the index and so catches a wrong *value* in the data, and ⅅ_def, which checks a quantity reached by two separate routes against itself and so catches a wrong *derivation*.
