## REWRITTEN

## Preface — the shape of the argument

This book makes one claim, shows it on one object, and reports every place the demonstration failed.

**The claim.** An index that is *closed* — closed under the meet and join of its own coordinates, meaning that combining any two entries by taking the larger value in each slot, or the smaller, always lands on another entry already in the index — cannot help but contain its own definition, cannot help but contain its own contradiction, and settles exactly what may be added to it. These are not three lucky properties that a well-made index happens to have. They are consequences of a single structural condition, and each of them fails in a specific, measurable way when that condition does not hold.

**Stated exactly, after every mechanism was examined one at a time.** A closed index is a *fixed point* of the operator ℛ — that is, the index equals its own reconstruction: rebuild it from its cells' own bounds and nothing is added (ℛ is set out in Chapter 11; E, below, is the count of what a reconstruction would add). **Being a fixed point gives six things and no more**: the index's alphabet, its bounds, the completeness of its membership test, which cells may be added, which axes may be adjoined, and which constraints may be imposed. **Three further powers are available, and not one of them follows from closure**: the coordinate order can be recovered, but only across a tree; a wrong value can be caught, but only from outside the index; a wrong derivation can be caught, but only along a second, independent route. **And the one thing closure never gives, at any price, is that the coordinates refer to anything in the world** — which is why Chapter 16 carries six defences rather than three, and why one of the six cannot be run by this book at all.

**And an index that is not a fixed point is not thereby broken.** Two cases have to be told apart. Sometimes a certificate can be shown — a relabelling of the coordinate system that turns the index into a closed one. Where such a relabelling exists, the gap the index shows is an artefact of how it was drawn, and E is measuring the *drawing*. Where no such relabelling exists, the gap is real, and E is measuring the *world*. **Openness is a measurement, and closure is simply the case where the measurement comes out zero.** This is why Chapter 15's thesis and the law of §18.4.1 turn out to be a single operator read in two directions — one reads it as "can this be relabelled into a closed object?", the other as "how far from closed is it?" — and it is why neither could be sharpened from inside the other. Register 428.

**The object.** Λ, an ordering of atomic electron configurations on eight coordinates. It holds no physics that was not already in the quantum numbers; it is a recoding — the same information, written a different way. That is on purpose. A recoding adds nothing, so whatever Λ then displays is a property of *indexing*, not of atoms.

**The demonstration.** A method that brackets Rydberg energy levels — brackets meaning it places each level inside an interval built only from its measured neighbours, with no model of the atom. It is tested on 1,442 interior cells across 35 atomic systems, spanning orbital angular momentum ℓ = 0 to 6 and nuclear charge *Z* = 1 to 83. The bracket holds on every one. That is a weaker result than it sounds, and Chapter 25 explains why — and then shows what the method's silence is actually measuring.

**The register.** One thousand six hundred and twenty-nine claims made in the course of this work were wrong, and were withdrawn. They are listed in Chapter 28, each with what replaced it. Several were caught not by the author but by the object itself: a wrong nuclear charge the method could not see, and two derived quantities that flagged it at once. That episode is the book's best evidence, and it is the account of an error.

### Why the periodic table is the right place to start

The periodic table is the most successful index in the physical sciences. It is also not closed, and the cost of that is exactly thirty-six cells.

Chapter 6 computes it. Read the table as coordinates — period and group — and take the bounds its occupied cells imply. Those bounds admit period 1 group 2, period 2 group 3, and thirty-four others. None of them exists. **You have to be told that period 1 holds only two elements; the table cannot tell you itself.** That is what it means for an index to be open: it admits cells that are not there, and it needs an outside voice to rule them out.

Λ is the same 118 elements. The cost of ruling out what it does not contain, from its own structure, is zero.

That difference is the whole book. Everything in Part II builds the object, everything in Part III proves why the difference exists, and everything in Part V tests what such an object can and cannot do with real measurements.

### How to read this

If you want the general result, Part III stands on its own. Chapter 14 defines closure; Chapters 15 to 17 prove the three consequences; Chapter 18 states what none of it can deliver.

**If you want the physics**, Part V stands alone, given the definition of the bracket in Chapter 22.

If you want to check the work, Chapter 28 and Appendix B are where the withdrawals and the provenance live. Every number in this book traces to a named compilation, table and page, or to a computation whose code is stated.

**And if you are looking for a prediction**, there is none — and Chapter 25 explains why. What looks like a prediction is a deduction from measured neighbours, offered as something falsifiable rather than as a result. **The rest of the book is about why an index cannot supply more of them.** §25.6 gives the reason in one line: the number of predictions an index can make is E(X), the count of cells its own structure implies but does not list — and a complete index has E(X) = 0. It has no unlisted cells to propose.

**That is not a limitation discovered late. It is what completeness means.** An index may be complete, or it may be predictive; it cannot be both. A prediction is a proposal about an unlisted cell, and completeness is exactly the property of having none.
