## Appendix F — The numbers, indexed

This appendix indexes the **kinds of quantity** this work produces and the **ordered grounds** it
produces them over. It does not index the numbers this book prints about itself. That was the
domain the appendix first took, and it was the wrong one: an account of a volume's own printed
figures belongs to the making of the volume and not to what the volume is about. The construction
survived the change of domain; the readings did not.

What is indexed here is therefore a structure and not a table of results. Every figure this work
states lives in its own chapter, and the Register carries the entry that established it. What this
appendix supplies is the standard those statements are held to, the shape they take when a quantity
is fibred over an order, and the one case where a quantity may not be stated as a figure at all.

### F.1 The population

A quantity in this work is never loose. It arrives as a **kind** carried at a **point of a ground**,
and both must be named before the number means anything. Six kinds recur across the volumes.

| kind | what it counts | the ground it is carried over |
|---|---|---|
| **extent** | admitted cells of an index | the tower, as a chain |
| **defect** | the departure of a level from its hydrogenic position | the series ground, ordered by principal number |
| **bound** | the occupancy a shell admits | the periodic ground, ordered by proton number |
| **share** | a subset of a population against that population | whichever ground carries the population |
| **depth** | distance from a root in a dependence order | the dependence order of the compendia |
| **grade** | the warrant a statement carries | the audit hierarchy |

The list is closed by construction rather than by survey: a kind enters it when some ground in this
work carries that kind at every one of its points, and leaves it when no ground does. A seventh
candidate is refused below at F.4.3.

### F.2 The fibring of a quantity-kind over an ordered ground

Let a ground be a set *P* with a partial order ≤, and let *K* be a quantity-kind. The **fibring** of
*K* over *P* assigns to each *p* in *P* the set *K(p)* of values of that kind admitted at *p*. The
assignment is not free. It is a fibring of *K* over *P* only when the order is carried:

  (i)  **the fibre is total** — every point of the ground admits a value, so that a gap in the
       fibre is a fact about the ground and never about the reading;
  (ii) **the order is respected** — where *p* ≤ *q*, the admissions at *p* constrain those at *q*
       in a way stated with the fibring and not discovered afterwards;
  (iii) **the fibre is a function of the ground alone** — two points that are indistinguishable in
       the ground admit the same values.

Clause (iii) is the load-bearing one, and it is a claim that can fail. It holds on the periodic
ground for the multiplicities admitted at a species: the values admitted at a proton number and
charge are a function of the electron count alone, on every pair the coordinate file carries and
with no exception (register 1779). It fails wherever a quantity is carried at a point by a source
rather than by the ground — which is why provenance is a column of the coordinate table and not a
remark about it.

A fibring that satisfies (i)–(iii) is what this work means by an **index**. A fibring that fails
(i) is a survey. One that fails (ii) is a list. One that fails (iii) is a table of readings, and it
may be perfectly correct without being an index at all. The distinction is not pedantry: only an
index composes with another index, and composition is what the tower is built out of.

### F.3 The index, built

A quantity in this work is stated to one standard, and the standard has four columns. A statement
that carries fewer than four is not a weaker version of the same claim; it is a different kind of
object, and the chapters that cite this section cite it for that reason.

| column | what it requires | what its absence costs |
|---|---|---|
| **the method** | the procedure named, not described | the reading cannot be repeated |
| **the input set** | the population named and bounded | the reading has no denominator |
| **the inputs** | every input printed, not summarised | the reading cannot be checked |
| **the refutation** | the test that would kill the claim | the reading cannot be wrong, and so says nothing |

The fourth column is the one this work added and the one it defends. A claim that names no test
that would refute it is not a claim about the ground; it is a description of the reading. Chapters
6, 12 and 24 state their central measurements to this standard explicitly and say so where they do.

**A constraint refuted by its own extent is dead.** This is F.3's rule and it is the fourth column
turned on the constraint itself. If a constraint is asserted over a ground, and computing its extent
on that ground returns the empty set or the whole of it, the constraint has been refuted by the only
test that could reach it — not weakened, not pending, dead. The work applies this without exception,
and several of the corrections the Register carries are exactly this rule firing.

### F.3.3 The closure, represented as it must be

One class of quantity cannot be stated as a figure, and the reason is structural rather than
practical.

Let a closure be computed over a domain, and let the statement of that count enter the domain. Then
the count is a quantity whose extent includes its own counting. Writing it changes the thing
counted: the figure is correct at the instant before it is written and false at the instant after,
and no amount of care in the measuring repairs it, because the fault is in the domain and not in the
reading. Such a count is **unstable by construction**.

The representation such a quantity must take instead is its **constraint** together with its
**refutations**. The rule, stated as the chapters cite it:

> **Assert the law, compute the extent.**

The law is stable because it does not live in the domain it governs. The extent is recomputed
whenever it is wanted and is never printed as a property of the object. Where no law is available,
a closure may still be carried **by the stable structure** — the shape that holds still under the
motion of the count — and this work names the cells where that structure holds rather than the
number they hold. Chapter 2's distinction between what is held by the law and what is held by the
stable structure rests here, and the two routes are not interchangeable: the first is exact, the
second is only as good as the stillness it claims.

The test of whether a quantity belongs to this class is short. **Would writing the number down
change it?** If it would, the number is not the object; the constraint is.

### F.4.3 Time, which this work has and does not read

A date is admitted nowhere in Λ, and the refusal is the seventh candidate kind of F.1 being turned
away.

A date is a coordinate of the **recording**, not of the thing recorded. It orders the acts of
measurement; it does not order the ground. Admitting it as a kind would satisfy F.2 (i) and (ii)
trivially — every reading has a time, and times are totally ordered — and would fail (iii)
absolutely, since two points indistinguishable in the ground routinely carry different dates for no
reason in the ground at all. A date therefore indexes the observer, and an index of the observer
composed with an index of the object yields neither.

The consequence is a prohibition and not a preference: **no cell of any index in this work is keyed
by a date.** Where the work needs to read an order of events, it reads the Register, which is the
record of the making and is ordered by exactly that clock. The two are kept apart on purpose. This
is the one place where the work states what it will not index, and it states it here because F.2's
third clause is the reason.
