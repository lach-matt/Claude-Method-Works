### How far the method reaches

The method is one move, made over and over: build an index from what was observed, close it under its own rules, and read what the closure costs. The rest of this book is what that move yields when you keep making it.

It yields the defect first. Take the periodic table at its word and it costs thirty-six — thirty-six cells its own coordinates admit and it denies. Rebuild the same elements on coordinates that carry their own constraints and the cost falls to zero. That is the method paying off once: a number where there had been only a diagram.

Keep making the move and it keeps paying. The zero-cost index is not the end of a tower but the bottom of one — each higher index adds a coordinate, closes again, and costs nothing again, up through a thirteenth. Along the way the same move returns things it was never asked for: a clock, in the constraint that says you cannot place more than you removed; a direction of time that holds or dissolves depending on what an observer takes as the starting state; a proof that a structure holding both directions at once cannot be closed at any price. None of these was put in. They fell out of building an index and reading it.

And at the top the move reaches past what it built. The tower stops at thirteen because the physics stops being supplied, not because the construction fails — and the index can still say something about the fourteenth coordinate it does not have. It can bound the direction: an object occupying less and less of the space it is written in while defending itself with more and more redundancy. It cannot name that coordinate and does not pretend to. But being able to point at a dimension you never built, and say which way it lies, is the method's furthest payoff — and it lands exactly where the book began, on a defined object at the edge of what can be observed. The seed the work opened on and the horizon it reaches are the same point.

That edge is worth naming plainly, because it is where the method stops. The method reaches everything that can be observed: it says what a thing is, completely, and where it must have been between two observations. It does not say what a thing will become — that is prediction, and this book does not claim it. What lies past the last observation, and what lies past the thirteenth index, are bounded and not built, named and not crossed. Definition comes from observing a thing from outside it; the method works up to that boundary and reports it honestly rather than reaching across it. That is not the subject's weakness. It is the oldest discipline in it.

So the four moves are the whole of what travels: build the index, close it, read the defect, record every failure. Everything else in this book — the tower, the clock, the two directions, the fourteenth axis — is those four moves, paying off. Whether they would pay off the same way in a universe no measurement can reach is the author's belief and is offered as one, named here and leaned on nowhere. What can be shown is shown, and it starts with a table that costs thirty-six.
